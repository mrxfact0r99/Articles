I am going to share with you an interesting and commonly ignored type of bug today. So let’s get started! 🐞🔍

I began by analyzing the structure of the target web application. This step is very important and often missed by many bug hunters. While exploring, I discovered a page named SupportForm.aspx. It had input fields like name, email, your query, some other options, and a submit button.

I usually start by sending a demo message to see how the form works. Then I tried sending blind SSRF and blind XSS payloads — but nothing worked. I assumed there was no bug and thought about moving on.

But suddenly, while I was eating 🍽️, an idea hit me 💡 — what happens if I submit the form using any random email address?

I tried it, and surprisingly, it got submitted and accepted successfully ✅.

Then I wondered — why not try sending it using an admin or company email address? I used OSINT to find some company emails and submitted the form again. And guess what? It worked! The message was sent successfully using their internal email address. 📬

So what is the bug?

There’s a twist here. The form was:

  • Accepting any email address, including internal or company ones.

This Means:

  • Attackers can impersonate company employees or customers and submit misleading or malicious requests (like asking for password resets, admin access, or sensitive info).
  • If internal teams respond to spoofed messages without verifying the sender, attackers might receive confidential information.
  • Combined with user enumeration, attackers can target known employees or customers with social engineering.
  • This seriously undermines the integrity of the support system. ⚠️

I reported it with this mindset, and the report was accepted in 10 days. I received a $50 reward. 🤑

The Takeaway: Bugs are everywhere — you just need to think like an attacker or hacker. Understand how the company works, and you’ll find vulnerabilities that many others miss. 🧠💥
